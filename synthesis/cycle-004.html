Theoretical implications

The cycle’s strongest theoretical implication concerns what an observation can close. Under an unstable or contested classification, a realised event can be compatible with several mechanisms and several definitions of success. A multi-path field can preserve this ambiguity, but it does not resolve it. A useful orienting practice therefore pairs a bounded disposition with an explicit rule for what remains unknown after each observable arrives. The daily corpus documents this design problem; it does not estimate the superiority of one epistemic stance.

The LLM-cognition implication is correspondingly narrow. Valid-looking field names can coexist with semantic drift, and a fluent terminal sentence can test a different proposition from the opening hypothesis. Those are observable features of generated text, not measurements of an internal cognitive mechanism. Identifying mean-trajectory regression, reflexive blindness or mechanism–outcome decoupling requires comparisons that separate these accounts from ordinary evidence gaps. Neither the absence of resolved failures nor the presence of polished counterfactual prose does that work.

For human–AI analytical ensembles, the audit suggests that periodic reframing must sometimes change the unit of evaluation itself. The move from event forecasts to research propositions makes an unchanged forecast score misleading; the move from new anomaly cards to carried registers makes unchanged counts misleading. Human judgement adds value when it notices that the instrument is measuring a different object. The six research designs below turn that observation into questions that can be tested rather than treating this corpus as model validation.
